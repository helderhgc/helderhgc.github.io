Who should we partner with in the race to net zero?

A beginner's guide to the paper that combines network analysis with the PROMETHEE II method to decide which countries are the most attractive partners in net-zero technology projects. No prerequisites: if you know what a circle of friends is and how a league table works, you can follow along.

Linhares, L. F.; Costa, H. G.; Eufrazio, E. P. Integrating Social Network Analysis and PROMETHEE II for Prioritizing International Cooperation Partners in Net-Zero Technology Projects. Universidade Federal Fluminense, INSID 2026.

The paper in 30 seconds

The question

With limited resources, which countries are most worth deepening cooperation with to develop clean-energy technologies?

The method

First, a network of who works with whom (168 projects, 53 countries). Then PROMETHEE II ranks 15 countries on three criteria.

The answer

United States, Germany, Japan, the Netherlands and China lead. If technological maturity matters most, South Korea, Canada and Denmark move up.

1The problem in plain words

To meet the Paris Agreement, countries need low-carbon technologies: hydrogen, CO₂ capture, batteries, offshore wind… Almost no country does this alone. The projects are run by multinational consortia.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) keeps a public database, the ETP Clean Energy Technology Guide, that records these projects, the countries taking part and how mature each technology is.

Imagine you work at a ministry or an innovation agency. Budget, diplomatic staff and time are limited. The practical question is: who should we invest in partnering with?

The paper answers in two stages:

Stage 1 · The network

Turns the IEA database into a map of who collaborates with whom and measures each country's position on that map.

→
Stage 2 · The decision

Uses those measures, plus technological maturity, as PROMETHEE II criteria to rank the countries.

2Stage 1: the cooperation network

A network (or graph) has two ingredients: nodes and links. Here each node is a country, and two countries are linked when they take part in at least one project together. With the IEA's 168 multinational projects, the network has 53 countries and 470 links.

As in a circle of friends, a few countries are very well connected (the hubs) and many sit on the periphery. To measure each country's position, the paper uses two centralities:

Degree centrality: how many doors a country opens. A has 6 direct partners, but all of them are peripheral.

Eigenvector centrality: how close a country sits to the influential core. B has only 2 partners, but they are hubs.

  • C1 · Degree centrality: the number of direct partners divided by the maximum possible (52). It measures breadth of access. The United States has 47 partners: 47/52 = 0.904.
  • C2 · Eigenvector centrality: a country is influential when it is linked to other influential countries, as in “you are known by the company you keep”. It measures closeness to the core of the network.

The third criterion: technological maturity (TRL)

TRL (Technology Readiness Level) is a scale created by NASA that runs from 1 (an idea on paper) to 9 (a technology sold and running commercially).

C3 · Average TRL: the average TRL of the projects a country takes part in. It measures portfolio maturity, that is, how close to market the technologies a partnership gives access to are.

Be careful with the TRL average

TRL is an ordinal scale: the steps are ordered, but they are not equal distances. The authors use the average because it is practical and common, but they warn that two countries with the same average can have very different portfolios.

3The key finding: you can't have it all

Do the most connected countries have the most mature technologies? No. The chart shows the opposite: countries with more partners have, on average, less mature portfolios.

Each dot is a country (hover to see its name). Spearman correlation ρ = −0.66 (p < 0.01). Brazil in orange. Data: Table 1 of the paper.

The explanation is intuitive: a country that joins many projects also joins many early-stage ones, which pulls its average down. South Korea, Canada and Brazil take part in fewer projects, but more mature ones.

The consequence: choosing partners by a single indicator is misleading. Breadth of access, influence and maturity pull in different directions. This is exactly the kind of conflict that multi-criteria methods exist for.

Other findings: eigenvector centrality is practically unrelated to TRL (ρ = −0.12), and the two centralities are only moderately associated. Each criterion carries its own information.

5Stage 2: PROMETHEE II step by step

Think of a round-robin league: each country “plays” each of the other 14. In every match, we measure how much one beats the other on each criterion. At the end, what counts is the goal difference.

  1. Difference on each criterion. For the pair (a, b) and criterion j: dj = gj(a) − gj(b).
  2. Preference degree on each criterion. The difference becomes a number between 0 and 1 through a V-shape function: if a loses, it is 0; if a wins, it is the difference divided by the criterion's range (largest value minus smallest).
    Pj(a, b) = max(0, dj) ÷ rangej

    Winning by the whole range is worth 1; winning by half of it is worth 0.5.
  3. Global outranking index. Combine the criteria with the weights (which add up to 1):
    π(a, b) = Σ wj · Pj(a, b)

    It is how much a beats b considering all criteria.1
  4. Flows. Average over all opponents:
    • φ⁺ (leaving flow): how much the country beats the others, like goals scored.
    • φ⁻ (entering flow): how much the country is beaten, like goals conceded.
    • φ = φ⁺ − φ⁻ (net flow): the goal difference.
  5. Ranking. Sort the countries from highest to lowest φ. A positive balance means the country outranks more than it is outranked.

1 In the PROMETHEE literature, π(a, b) is usually called the “aggregated preference index”. Here “preference” is reserved for the comparison on a single criterion and “outranking” for the global comparison.

7What the results show

The baseline ranking (equal weights)

  • The United States leads by a wide margin (φ = 0.347): it is the strongest on both centralities and has a mid-range TRL.
  • Next come Germany (0.158), Japan (0.110), the Netherlands (0.088) and China (0.077).
  • South Korea has the most polarized profile: high φ⁺ and high φ⁻ at the same time, because it has the highest TRL but sits on the periphery of the network.
  • Brazil is 14th: its high TRL (7.50) does not make up for the lowest eigenvector centrality in the group (0.044). Brazil takes part in mature projects, but far from the influential core.

Two partner archetypes emerge:

  • Gateway hubs (United States, Germany, Netherlands, China, Japan): attractive whenever access to the network matters.
  • Maturity specialists (South Korea, Canada, Denmark): attractive to those who want near-market technologies. In scenario S4, South Korea climbs from 8th to 1st, and Germany drops from 2nd to 9th.

8Can we trust the result? The robustness checks

  • A different method, TOPSIS. TOPSIS ranks each country by its distance to an “ideal” and an “anti-ideal” country. The two rankings agree closely (ρ = 0.85). The differences appear in the middle of the table, where net flows are close to zero; the United Kingdom, for example, is 11th under PROMETHEE II and 6th under TOPSIS.
  • A different preference function. Replacing the V-shape with the usual function changes almost nothing (ρ = 0.971): the leader is the same and only France replaces Japan in the top five. Try it in the simulator.
  • Removing one country at a time. In 12 of the 15 removals, the order of the rest does not change. The exceptions are the United States, South Korea and Brazil, precisely the countries that set the range of some criterion. Even then, the top five never changes in the baseline scenario.
  • Shrinking or enlarging the set. With only the 10 most connected countries, the order repeats. In a simulated larger set, the United States, Germany, Japan and China always stay in the top five.
Robustness is not validation

PROMETHEE II and TOPSIS reason differently. Their agreement increases confidence, but it does not prove the ranking is “right”, and disagreement would not prove it wrong.

9What does this mean for Brazil?

Brazil has a mature portfolio but little influence in the network. The model suggests a two-track strategy:

  • Consolidate ties with the hubs (United States, Germany, Japan), which give indirect access to the rest of the network and to the main knowledge flows.
  • Explore agreements with the maturity specialists (South Korea, Canada, Denmark), whose portfolios match deployment priorities.

When Brazil is removed from the list (it cannot partner with itself), the order of the other 14 barely changes: only Italy and the United Kingdom swap places. Try it in the simulator by choosing “Brazil” under “Remove a country”.

A shortlist, not the final answer

The criteria describe each country in the global network, not its relationship with Brazil. Choosing a bilateral partner also requires pair-specific criteria: technological complementarity, existing agreements and institutional compatibility.

10Limitations the authors point out

  • Only the 15 most connected countries were evaluated, and that selection used one of the criteria itself (degree).
  • No real decision maker was consulted: the weights are scenarios, and each ranking holds for its own scenario.
  • The network is unweighted (a link counts the same with 1 or 10 joint projects) and is a snapshot of a single moment.
  • The average TRL hides the distribution of projects and differences across sectors.
  • With the V-shape function and p equal to the range, PROMETHEE II becomes fully compensatory (equivalent to a weighted sum of normalized scores). That is why the usual function was also tested.

Future work: evaluate all 53 countries, use a weighted network, add other centralities (betweenness, closeness), measure maturity by the full TRL distribution and elicit the weights directly from policymakers.

11Quick glossary

Net zero
Net-zero emissions: the CO₂ emitted is balanced by the CO₂ removed.
SNA (social network analysis)
A set of techniques for studying who is connected to whom in a network.
Graph
A network made of nodes (here, countries) and links (joint projects).
Degree centrality
The share of the other countries a country cooperates with directly.
Eigenvector centrality
A country's importance measured by the importance of its partners.
TRL
A 1-to-9 scale of technology maturity, from basic research to market.
Criterion
A point of view used to compare the alternatives.
Preference degree Pj(a, b)
From 0 to 1, how much a is preferred to b on a single criterion j.
Global outranking index π(a, b)
The weighted average of the preference degrees: how much a outranks b considering all criteria.
Flows φ⁺, φ⁻, φ
Strength, weakness and balance of each country against all the others.
Entropy
A way to derive weights from the data: the criterion that best separates the alternatives weighs more.
TOPSIS
A method that ranks by closeness to an ideal solution and distance from an anti-ideal one.
Spearman ρ
Correlation between two rankings: 1 means identical orders, −1 opposite orders.
Rank reversal
When adding or removing an alternative swaps the order of two others.

12Questions every beginner asks

Why not just rank by number of partners?

Because number of partners and maturity move in opposite directions in this data (ρ = −0.66). Ranking by a single indicator hides that conflict and always favors the same kind of country.

So the United States is “the best partner” for any country?

No. The ranking measures each country's general attractiveness in the network. For a specific country, you still need to look at technological complementarity, existing agreements and institutional compatibility.

Who chose the weights?

Nobody in particular, on purpose. The five scenarios cover plausible policy orientations. The useful result is knowing which countries do well in every scenario (the hubs) and which depend on the profile (the maturity specialists).

Why can removing one country change the order of the others?

Because the preference degree is divided by the criterion's range. If the removed country had the highest or lowest value on a criterion, the range changes and so do the comparisons among the rest. That is why only the United States, South Korea and Brazil change the order when removed.

Isn't PROMETHEE II an outranking, non-compensatory method?

It is an outranking method, but how compensatory it is depends on the preference functions. With the V-shape function and threshold p equal to the range, the net flow is equivalent to a weighted sum of normalized scores, so it becomes compensatory. The usual function reduces this effect, and the paper shows the ranking barely changes with it.
